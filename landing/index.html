Why it matters

Delirium looks like a bad day.

It's a sudden change in thinking, often set off by something ordinary: an infection, dehydration, a new medicine or surgery. Caught early, the cause can often be treated. The most common kind in older adults is the quiet kind, sleepy and withdrawn, and it's easy to miss over a Sunday phone call.

Up to 1 in 2older adults in hospital have delirium at some point during their stay.1

3 in 4cases in older emergency patients were missed by the doctors treating them.2

30–40%of cases can be prevented, which is why noticing early matters.1

How it works

Two minutes a day, and one plain answer.

The questions are adapted from the 4AT, a short bedside test clinicians already use to screen for delirium. Clari asks them out loud and compares each answer with that person's own normal days.

  1. 1You, once

    Set it up in about a minute

    Add a few details, like their birthday and town, so Clari knows the right answers. Their phone joins with a short code.

  2. 2Them, every day

    They answer by talking

    Clari reads each question aloud: their age, the year, the months backwards. No typing. The wording changes daily so nobody learns the answers.

  3. 3Clari

    It compares them with their usual

    Answers are scored the 4AT way. Their pace, pauses and how quickly they reply are compared with their own last few normal days, never with anyone else.

  4. 4You, the same day

    You get one clear result

    With the reasons in plain words and their recorded answers to listen to. If they skip a day, you get a text, because withdrawing can be a sign too.

What you'll see

Three results, each with its reasons.

Their usual self

0 points

Answers and voice look like their normal days. It can't rule out illness, so trust your own sense too.

Keep an eye on it

1 to 3 points, or a missed day

Something is a little off, or answers were hard to hear. Give them a call and see how they are.

Call their doctor

4 points or more

The kind of change the 4AT treats as possible delirium. Call their doctor today, and in an emergency call 911.

Points follow the 4AT's own scale. In clinical studies, the 4AT given by clinicians caught 88% of delirium cases across 17 studies.3 Clari's spoken, at-home version hasn't been tested that way yet.

Made for older ears and hands

Easy enough to do every single day.

If you're the one checking in: you start it when you're ready, it takes about two minutes, and you can ask to hear any question again.

  • Questions read aloud, answers spokenOne question per screen, a big "Hear it again" button, and it moves on by itself after a few seconds of quiet.
  • Large, high-contrast typeBody text starts at 22 points and every colour meets a 7:1 contrast ratio. Results always use a symbol and a word, never colour alone.
  • It listens to the room firstA TV in the background was the biggest cause of false alarms in our tests, so Clari asks for a quieter spot before it starts.
  • Tuned for older voicesWe fine-tuned an open speech model on tens of thousands of recordings of people over sixty, so "eighty-two" isn't heard as "eighty too".

"Start my check-in"One button. About two minutes.

Privacy

No cameras. Nobody watching.

Many older adults don't want to be watched in their own home, and they shouldn't have to be. Clari only listens for two minutes, when they choose to start.

Voice only

No camera and no always-on microphone. It records only the answers during a check-in.

Our own speech model

Answers are transcribed by our own model on our own server, not sent to an outside company.

Family only

Only the family they're linked with can hear the recordings.

Theirs to control

Recording sharing can be switched off, and recordings can be deleted.

Where Clari is today

A working prototype, and an honest list.

Built

  • A working Android test app for the older person and the family, plus the server that transcribes and scores each check-in.
  • The 4AT's four parts by voice: alertness, orientation, attention and sudden change, with an optional evening check-in.
  • A speech model fine-tuned on older voices, the quiet-room check, and texts for missed check-ins.

Not yet

  • Clari hasn't been tested with people who have delirium. Its voice checks have only seen recordings of healthy speakers and simulated changes.
  • It isn't in the App Store or Google Play, and there's no iPhone build yet.
  • It's a screening aid, not a diagnosis, and not a medical device.

Next: a pilot with hospitals and delirium researchers, comparing Clari's results with clinicians' own 4AT scores after patients go home.

Questions

Things families ask.

What if they just have a cold or slept badly?

Clari compares each day with their own recent normal days, so one off morning usually shows up as "Keep an eye on it", not an alarm. The voice measures alone can't trigger "Call their doctor". You can also listen to their answers yourself.

Does "their usual self" mean they're fine?

No. It means today's answers look like their normal days. Clari can miss things, so if you're worried, call them or their doctor anyway.

What if they don't do the check-in?

If they haven't checked in a few hours after their reminder, you get a text. A missed day never shows as a good day.

What phone does it need?

Today there's an Android test version. It isn't in the app stores yet, and there's no iPhone version yet.

Who can hear the recordings?

Only the family members linked to that person. Sharing the recordings can be turned off.

Working on delirium or hospital discharge?

We're looking for hospitals and researchers to pilot Clari with families after a hospital stay. The code is open.
